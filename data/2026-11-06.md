### [1] [Slide to Report: Continuous Reporting Symptom-Specific Cybersickness in VR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07345)
*Purnata Saha,Rifatul Islam,Qiliang He,John Quarles*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper evaluates whether three concurrent symptom-specific cybersickness ratings—nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation—provide more informative measurements during VR than one overall sickness rating. In a 52-participant VR rollercoaster study, the three-slider method aligned more specifically with corresponding SSQ subscales without harming usability, workload, or attention, and the authors released a multimodal physiological dataset.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Conventional overall cybersickness ratings are too coarse to distinguish symptom types or track their temporal evolution, while post-exposure instruments such as the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire cannot be administered during immersion. The study therefore seeks a continuous, in-experience assessment method that preserves symptom specificity without imposing excessive cognitive or usability costs.

Method: A between-subjects experiment with 52 participants compared a three-slider interface for continuously reporting nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation against a single-slider overall sickness interface during a VR rollercoaster. Participants also performed an auditory Simon task to assess attention. The study collected galvanic skin response, electrocardiography, photoplethysmography, eye tracking, and head-tracking data, and compared in-experience ratings with SSQ symptom subscales after exposure.

Result: Each of the three symptom-specific sliders showed the strongest association with its corresponding SSQ subscale, indicating greater symptom specificity. The single slider correlated with all SSQ subscales, suggesting that it captured undifferentiated overall sickness. The three-slider interface did not significantly impair usability, increase workload, or reduce attention. The resulting multimodal dataset was publicly released.

Conclusion: Continuous three-dimensional symptom reporting is a more informative alternative to a single overall cybersickness rating during VR exposure, while remaining practical and non-disruptive. The released dataset may facilitate physiological modeling and further research on the dynamics and detection of cybersickness.

Abstract: Cybersickness is often assessed during VR exposure through discrete overall sickness, which cannot capture specific symptoms or their changes over time. Post-exposure questionnaires such as the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire provide symptom-specific measures but cannot be collected during immersion. We investigate whether separating cybersickness into three symptom-specific dimensions -- nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation -- during immersion provides more informative continuous measurements. We conducted a between-subjects study with 52 participants in a VR-rollercoaster, comparing a three-slider interface with a single-slider interface. Concurrently, participants also completed an auditory Simon task to measure attention. We additionally collected multimodal set of physiological signals, including GSR, ECG, PPG, eye and head tracking. Results show that each slider correlated most strongly with its corresponding SSQ subscale, whereas the single slider correlated with all SSQ subscales. Using three sliders did not significantly affect usability, workload, or attention. We publicly release the multimodal dataset to support future cybersickness research.

### [2] [Breaking the Modality Barrier: Multimodal Fusion With Missing Modalities for Cybersickness Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07386)
*Purnata Saha,Rifatul Islam,John Quarles*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper presents a transformer-based cybersickness prediction model that remains effective with arbitrary combinations of available sensors. It aligns heterogeneous sensor representations through eye-tracking-anchored contrastive learning and uses masked self-attention to ignore missing modalities. Evaluation on VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster shows strong robustness to sensor dropout and improved cross-dataset generalization.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Cybersickness limits comfortable virtual-reality use, but practical prediction systems cannot assume that every physiological or behavioral sensor is always available. Existing fixed-modality models are fragile under sensor loss and often generalize poorly across datasets collected with different hardware and sensor configurations. The paper therefore seeks a modality-flexible and cross-dataset prediction approach that avoids imputing missing sensor data.

Method: The authors adapt a transformer architecture to accept arbitrary sensor combinations. Each sensor modality is embedded separately, and anchor-based contrastive learning aligns heterogeneous embeddings in a shared latent space, using eye tracking as the anchor modality. During multimodal fusion, masked self-attention excludes unavailable sensors rather than replacing them through imputation. The method is evaluated on three public datasets—VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster—with different VR systems and sensor configurations, including single-sensor dropout and joint cross-dataset training settings.

Result: Under single-sensor dropout, the prediction error changes by less than 0.09 MAE, indicating strong robustness to missing modalities. Joint training across all three datasets achieves 0.51 MAE and a Pearson correlation of 0.63, substantially outperforming models trained on individual datasets. These findings indicate improved generalization across heterogeneous VR platforms and sensing setups.

Conclusion: The proposed modality-flexible transformer provides robust cybersickness prediction without imputing missing sensors. Contrastive alignment and masked attention enable effective fusion across changing sensor configurations, while joint multi-dataset training improves generalization. The results support the approach as a practical solution for real-world VR systems in which sensor availability varies.

Abstract: Cybersickness is a major barrier to comfortable VR use, and reliable prediction often requires multiple physiological and behavioral sensors. However, existing models typically assume a fixed set of modalities, making them vulnerable when sensors are unavailable and difficult to generalize across datasets with different sensor configurations. We adapt a transformer-based model that handles arbitrary sensor combinations without imputing missing data. Anchor-based contrastive learning maps heterogeneous sensor embeddings into a shared latent space using eye tracking as the anchor modality, while masked self-attention excludes unavailable sensors during fusion. We evaluate our approach on three publicly available cybersickness datasets: VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster, collected using different VR systems and sensor configurations. Across single-sensor dropout conditions, prediction error changes by less than 0.09 MAE. Joint training across all datasets achieves 0.51 MAE and 0.63 Pearson correlation, substantially outperforming single-dataset models. These results demonstrate robust and generalizable cybersickness prediction across diverse VR systems.

### [3] [Thinking Is Not Writing: Designing AI Chatbots to Connect Reasoning and Revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07792)

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper presents Maike, a Socratic chatbot that supports argumentative writing by diagnosing arguments, asking targeted questions, and linking reflection in dialogue to essay revision. <details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: AI writing support often focuses on generating or correcting text, while effective argumentative writing also requires reflection on ideas and reasoning. The paper addresses the need to understand and design AI systems that connect students’ reflective dialogue with concrete revision processes and educational outcomes.

Method: The authors developed Maike with argument diagnosis, targeted Socratic questioning, a student model, and Think and Write modes. They conducted a classroom study with 65 secondary-school students comparing Maike with a control chatbot that used the same interface, language model, and modes. They analyzed essay-score changes, dialogue content, and transitions between discussed ideas and revisions.

Result: Approximately 40% of students improved their essay scores. Maike users produced more idea-level dialogue than control users (56% versus 44%) and more often moved iteratively between discussion and editing. Transitions from ideas discussed with the chatbot to subsequent essay revisions were positively associated with score gains.

Conclusion: AI writing support should be designed to coordinate reflection with revision rather than simply generate essay text. The paper contributes an architecture for supporting argumentative reasoning and an analytical approach for linking interaction patterns to writing processes and learning outcomes.

Abstract: Supporting students' argumentative writing with AI requires connecting reflection to their revision processes. We present Maike, a Socratic chatbot supporting writing through argument diagnosis, targeted questioning, and a student model across Think and Write modes. In a classroom study, 65 secondary-school students used Maike or a control chatbot sharing the interface, language model, and Think/Write modes. Approximately 40% of students improved their essay scores. However, Maike users engaged more in idea-level dialogue (56% vs. 44%) and moved iteratively between discussion and editing. Transitions from ideas discussed in dialogue to revisions were positively associated with score gains. These findings suggest a design target for AI writing support: coordinating reflection with revision. We contribute an architecture for supporting argumentative reasoning without generating essay text and an approach linking interaction patterns to writing processes and outcomes. As AI becomes embedded in classrooms, understanding these connections is essential to assessing (and designing for) its educational value.

### [5] [Tasteply: Externalizing Stable and Situational Preference as Manipulable Spatial Objects](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07851)
*DaEun Choi,Daniel Lee*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper presents Tasteply, an interactive recommender-system interface that makes stable and situational user preferences visible and manipulable as spatial objects. An exploratory study with five participants suggests that this spatial interaction can help users steer recommendations, while also revealing usability and conceptual limitations.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Most recommender systems model user preference as a unified, latent signal, even though real preferences include both stable tendencies and context-dependent interests. Because these components are hidden inside the recommendation model, users have limited ability to understand or directly control why particular content is recommended. The paper is motivated by the need for more transparent and user-steerable preference representations.

Method: Tasteply externalizes the preference structure into a spatial interface. Situational preferences are represented as attractors, while stable preference is represented as an underlying global field. Users manipulate recommendations by navigating the space and adjusting the strength of the global field, thereby directly interacting with preference components that are normally latent in the recommender system. The authors evaluate the concept through an exploratory user study with N=5 participants.

Result: The study found that the spatial control mechanism was effective for manipulating preference-based recommendations. It also identified limitations and opportunities for improvement, although the abstract does not specify the detailed findings, evaluation metrics, or the nature of those limitations.

Conclusion: Making stable and situational preferences spatially visible and manipulable is a promising approach to improving user control over recommender systems. Tasteply demonstrates the feasibility of this interaction paradigm, but the small exploratory sample means that stronger conclusions about general effectiveness require further research with larger and more diverse studies.

Abstract: Most recommender systems treat user preferences as a single signal to infer and optimize, yet human preferences are a mix of a stable preference and situational preferences that turn on and off depending on context. Through Tasteply, we externalize this preference structure as manipulable spatial objects. Tasteply represents each situational preference as an attractor and the stable preference as an underlying global field. Users can adjust their feed by moving around the space and adjusting the strength of the global field, turning the preference structure that remains latent within the model into something they can see and act on directly to steer the recommendation algorithm. Through an exploratory study (N=5), we found that this spatial control was effective, along with its limits and future direction as a means of manipulating preference-based recommendations.

TL;DR: IntentWeaver is a human-AI co-writing system that helps writers maintain coherence between high-level writing goals and low-level text edits. <details>
  <summary>Details</summary>

The paper addresses this breakdown, termed intent incoherence, to support more deliberate and coherent human-AI writing.

Method: The authors first conducted a formative study tracing how intent incoherence develops, from attention capture to silent drift and failed reconciliation. They then developed IntentWeaver, which infers an intent hierarchy from the draft and writer interactions, assesses each intent's fulfillment and coherence with intents at other levels after every change, and propagates the effects of edits across the hierarchy. The system was evaluated in a within-subjects study with 24 participants against a baseline.

Result: Compared with the baseline, participants using IntentWeaver revisited and revised higher-level intents more frequently, experienced fewer unnoticed deviations between intents and text, and produced writing that more effectively realized their stated intents.

Conclusion: IntentWeaver demonstrates that AI co-writing tools can support not only text generation but also the ongoing evolution and cross-level coherence of writer intent. The findings motivate design approaches that make intent relationships and emergent intents visible and actionable throughout the writing process.

Abstract: Writers hold intents at many levels, from overarching goals to concrete wording, and evolve them by periodically popping back up from the text to higher-level intents. Generative AI co-writing disrupts this practice. Dense drafts capture attention and trap writers in local editing; higher-level intents stall while lower-level ones drift from them unnoticed, a breakdown we term intent incoherence. Our formative study traced this breakdown from attention capture through silent drift to failed reconciliation. We present IntentWeaver, which supports intent evolution while sustaining coherence in human-AI co-writing. It derives an intent hierarchy from the draft and writer interactions, evaluates each intent's fulfillment and cross-level coherence at every change, and propagates consequences across intent levels. In a within-subjects study (N=24) against a baseline, participants using IntentWeaver revisited and evolved higher-level intents more often, with fewer unnoticed deviations, and produced texts better realizing them. We further discuss design implications for emergent intents.

TL;DR: The paper presents Souffleur, a mobile-first, speech-driven summary display designed to help users resume voice conversations after interruptions. In a 48-person between-subjects study, Souffleur performed comparably to a full chat transcript, despite showing only a few concise phrases.

  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Mobile devices increasingly support speech-based interaction, but their interfaces still mainly present text transcripts. After a context switch, users may struggle to identify the key points needed to resume a voice conversation. The paper therefore seeks a glanceable, compact display that supports conversational continuity, including autobiographical use and conversations involving external participants.

Method: The authors derived design goals from autobiographical use and sessions with external participants, implemented them in Souffleur, and evaluated the system in a between-subjects study with 48 participants. Souffleur was compared with a conventional chat transcript while participants followed and resumed short voice conversations. Outcomes included ease of following and resuming conversations, effort, attention, and recall.

Result: There was no reliable difference between Souffleur and the conventional transcript in ease of following or resuming conversations, effort, attention, or recall. Conclusion: A compact, speech-driven, glanceable display can support resuming short voice conversations about as effectively as a conventional transcript. However, the lack of statistically reliable advantages means the study does not establish superiority; further work should examine longer, more complex, and more varied conversations and assess when concise summaries are sufficient.

Abstract: The speech capabilities of mobile devices have grown, but their displays still rely on a text-based chat. When resuming a voice chat after a context switch, the last messages may not convey the key points of the conversation. Autobiographical use and sessions with external participants led to design goals for a mobile-first, speech-driven, glanceable display that helps users continue their reasoning from where they left off. We implement these goals in Souffleur and evaluate it in a between-subjects study with 48 participants. Compared with a conventional chat transcript, we found no reliable difference in how easily participants followed and resumed short voice conversations, nor in effort, attention or recall, although Souffleur reduces the conversation to a few short phrases.
