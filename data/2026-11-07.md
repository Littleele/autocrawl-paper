### [1] [Slide to Report: Continuous Reporting Symptom-Specific Cybersickness in VR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07345)
*Purnata Saha,Rifatul Islam,Qiliang He,John Quarles*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: A between-subjects VR-rollercoaster study with 52 participants shows that continuously rating nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation separately during immersion provides more symptom-specific information than a single overall cybersickness rating. The three-slider method aligns with corresponding SSQ subscales, does not significantly increase usability, workload, or attentional costs, and is accompanied by a publicly released multimodal physiological dataset.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: The study addresses a measurement gap in cybersickness research. A single overall sickness rating cannot distinguish different symptom types or track their evolution during VR exposure, while post-exposure instruments such as the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) cannot be administered continuously during immersion. The authors therefore seek a practical in-immersion method that provides symptom-specific, temporally continuous measurements without substantially disrupting the VR experience or participants' performance.

Method: The authors conducted a between-subjects experiment with 52 participants exposed to a VR rollercoaster. Participants used either a three-slider interface, separately rating nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation, or a single-slider interface for overall cybersickness. They also completed an auditory Simon task to assess attention. Post-exposure SSQ scores were collected for validation, and physiological and behavioral data were recorded, including galvanic skin response (GSR), electrocardiography (ECG), photoplethysmography (PPG), eye tracking, and head tracking.

Result: Each symptom-specific slider correlated most strongly with its corresponding SSQ subscale, supporting the construct validity of the three-dimensional measurement approach. The single slider correlated with all SSQ subscales, suggesting that it captured general sickness but did not distinguish symptom categories. The three-slider interface did not significantly impair usability, increase workload, or reduce attention relative to the single-slider interface. The study also produced and publicly released a multimodal dataset containing physiological and tracking signals for future research.

Conclusion: Separating cybersickness into nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation enables more informative continuous assessment during VR exposure than a single overall rating. The approach appears feasible and minimally disruptive, while the released dataset may facilitate development and validation of future cybersickness models. The findings support symptom-specific in-immersion measurement, although broader validation across VR scenarios and populations would strengthen generalizability.

Abstract: Cybersickness is often assessed during VR exposure through discrete overall sickness, which cannot capture specific symptoms or their changes over time. Post-exposure questionnaires such as the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire provide symptom-specific measures but cannot be collected during immersion. We investigate whether separating cybersickness into three symptom-specific dimensions -- nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation -- during immersion provides more informative continuous measurements. We conducted a between-subjects study with 52 participants in a VR-rollercoaster, comparing a three-slider interface with a single-slider interface. Concurrently, participants also completed an auditory Simon task to measure attention. We additionally collected multimodal set of physiological signals, including GSR, ECG, PPG, eye and head tracking. Results show that each slider correlated most strongly with its corresponding SSQ subscale, whereas the single slider correlated with all SSQ subscales. Using three sliders did not significantly affect usability, workload, or attention. We publicly release the multimodal dataset to support future cybersickness research.

### [2] [Breaking the Modality Barrier: Multimodal Fusion With Missing Modalities for Cybersickness Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07386)
*Purnata Saha,Rifatul Islam,John Quarles*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper presents a sensor-flexible transformer for cybersickness prediction that remains effective when physiological or behavioral sensors are missing. It uses eye tracking as an anchor for contrastive alignment and masked self-attention for modality-aware fusion. Across VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster, it shows stable single-sensor dropout performance and stronger cross-dataset generalization, reaching 0.51 MAE and 0.63 Pearson correlation under joint training.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Cybersickness prediction in virtual reality commonly depends on multiple heterogeneous sensors, but existing models usually require a fixed modality set. Consequently, they are fragile to sensor failure or unavailability and often transfer poorly across datasets collected with different VR systems and sensor configurations. The paper aims to develop a prediction model that can directly handle arbitrary sensor combinations without imputing missing data while preserving cross-dataset generalization.

Method: The authors adapt a transformer-based architecture for variable sensor inputs. Anchor-based contrastive learning aligns embeddings from heterogeneous sensors into a shared latent representation, using eye tracking as the anchor modality. During multimodal fusion, masked self-attention excludes unavailable sensors so that missing modalities do not need to be reconstructed or imputed. The method is evaluated on the VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster cybersickness datasets, including single-sensor dropout experiments and joint training across datasets.

Result: Under single-sensor dropout conditions, prediction error changes by less than 0.09 MAE, indicating robustness to missing inputs. Joint training on all three datasets achieves 0.51 MAE and a Pearson correlation of 0.63, substantially outperforming models trained separately on individual datasets. The results suggest improved transferability across different VR systems and sensor configurations.

Conclusion: The proposed sensor-flexible transformer provides robust and generalizable cybersickness prediction under incomplete and heterogeneous sensing conditions. Combining anchor-based contrastive alignment with masked attention reduces dependence on a fixed modality set and supports effective cross-dataset learning. The findings indicate that multimodal cybersickness models can remain reliable without imputing unavailable sensor data.

Abstract: Cybersickness is a major barrier to comfortable VR use, and reliable prediction often requires multiple physiological and behavioral sensors. However, existing models typically assume a fixed set of modalities, making them vulnerable when sensors are unavailable and difficult to generalize across datasets with different sensor configurations. We adapt a transformer-based model that handles arbitrary sensor combinations without imputing missing data. Anchor-based contrastive learning maps heterogeneous sensor embeddings into a shared latent space using eye tracking as the anchor modality, while masked self-attention excludes unavailable sensors during fusion. We evaluate our approach on three publicly available cybersickness datasets: VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster, collected using different VR systems and sensor configurations. Across single-sensor dropout conditions, prediction error changes by less than 0.09 MAE. Joint training across all datasets achieves 0.51 MAE and 0.63 Pearson correlation, substantially outperforming single-dataset models. These results demonstrate robust and generalizable cybersickness prediction across diverse VR systems.

### [3] [Thinking Is Not Writing: Designing AI Chatbots to Connect Reasoning and Revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07792)

TL;DR: The paper evaluates Maike, a Socratic AI chatbot that supports argumentative writing by diagnosing arguments, asking targeted questions, and linking reflective dialogue to revision. <details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: AI writing support often focuses on generating or correcting text, while effective argumentative writing also requires students to reflect on the quality of their ideas and reasoning. The paper addresses the need to understand and design AI interactions that connect such reflection to students’ concrete revision processes and learning outcomes.

Method: The authors developed Maike, a Socratic chatbot with argument diagnosis, targeted questioning, and a student model. It supports two modes: Think for reflective discussion and Write for composing or revising. They conducted a classroom study with 65 secondary-school students, comparing Maike with a control chatbot that used the same interface, language model, and Think/Write modes. They analyzed dialogue types, transitions between discussion and editing, and their associations with essay-score changes.

Result: Approximately 40% of students improved their essay scores. Compared with the control condition, Maike users engaged in more idea-level dialogue (56% versus 44%) and switched more iteratively between discussion and editing. Transitions in which ideas discussed in dialogue were subsequently reflected in revisions were positively associated with score gains.

Conclusion: AI writing support should be designed to coordinate reflection with revision rather than merely generate essay text. Maike demonstrates an architecture for supporting argumentative reasoning through Socratic interaction and a method for relating interaction patterns to writing processes and outcomes. The findings also indicate that successful educational AI evaluation should examine how students use the system, not only their final scores.

Abstract: Supporting students' argumentative writing with AI requires connecting reflection to their revision processes. Approximately 40% of students improved their essay scores. However, Maike users engaged more in idea-level dialogue (56% vs. 44%) and moved iteratively between discussion and editing. Transitions from ideas discussed in dialogue to revisions were positively associated with score gains. These findings suggest a design target for AI writing support: coordinating reflection with revision. We contribute an architecture for supporting argumentative reasoning without generating essay text and an approach linking interaction patterns to writing processes and outcomes. As AI becomes embedded in classrooms, understanding these connections is essential to assessing (and designing for) its educational value.

*DaEun Choi,Daniel Lee*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: Tasteply is an interactive recommender-system interface that externalizes a user’s stable and situational preferences as a manipulable spatial model. In a small exploratory study (N=5), participants found spatial control effective, while also revealing limitations and directions for future work.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Conventional recommender systems often model user preference as a single, opaque signal. This overlooks the distinction between stable tastes and situational preferences that may activate or deactivate depending on context, and it gives users limited visibility or direct control over how recommendations are shaped.

Method: The paper introduces Tasteply, which represents situational preferences as spatial attractors and stable preferences as an underlying global field. Users steer recommendations by navigating the preference space and adjusting the strength of the global field. The system was evaluated through an exploratory user study with five participants.

Result: The study found that spatial preference control was effective for manipulating preference-based recommendations. It also identified limitations of this interaction approach and generated implications and future directions for improving spatial preference management.

Conclusion: Tasteply demonstrates the potential of making latent preference structures visible and directly manipulable. Separating stable and situational preferences may improve user agency in recommender systems, although the small sample size means that the findings are preliminary and require validation through larger and more rigorous studies.

Abstract: Most recommender systems treat user preferences as a single signal to infer and optimize, yet human preferences are a mix of a stable preference and situational preferences that turn on and off depending on context. Through Tasteply, we externalize this preference structure as manipulable spatial objects. Tasteply represents each situational preference as an attractor and the stable preference as an underlying global field. Users can adjust their feed by moving around the space and adjusting the strength of the global field, turning the preference structure that remains latent within the model into something they can see and act on directly to steer the recommendation algorithm. Through an exploratory study (N=5), we found that this spatial control was effective, along with its limits and future direction as a means of manipulating preference-based recommendations.
