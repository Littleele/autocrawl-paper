### [1] [Slide to Report: Continuous Reporting Symptom-Specific Cybersickness in VR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07345)
*Purnata Saha,Rifatul Islam,Qiliang He,John Quarles*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: A between-subjects VR-rollercoaster study with 52 participants shows that continuously rating nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation separately during immersion provides more symptom-specific information than a single overall sickness slider. The three-slider method aligned with corresponding Simulator Sickness Questionnaire subscales without reducing usability, increasing workload, or impairing attention. The authors also released a multimodal physiological dataset.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Existing cybersickness assessment methods have complementary limitations: a single overall rating cannot distinguish symptom types or track their distinct temporal changes, while post-exposure questionnaires such as the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire cannot be administered during VR immersion. The study therefore examines whether continuous, symptom-specific ratings collected during immersion can provide more informative measurements without imposing excessive user burden or affecting task performance.

Method: The authors conducted a between-subjects experiment with 52 participants exposed to a VR rollercoaster. Participants used either a three-slider interface measuring nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation separately, or a single-slider interface measuring overall sickness. They concurrently performed an auditory Simon task to assess attention. The study also recorded multimodal physiological and behavioral signals, including galvanic skin response, electrocardiography, photoplethysmography, eye tracking, and head tracking. Continuous ratings were compared with post-exposure SSQ subscales, and the interfaces were evaluated for usability, workload, and attentional effects.

Result: Each symptom-specific slider correlated most strongly with its corresponding SSQ subscale, supporting the validity and specificity of the three-dimensional assessment. In contrast, the single overall slider correlated with all SSQ subscales, indicating that it conflated distinct symptom dimensions. The three-slider interface did not significantly change usability, workload, or attention relative to the single-slider condition. The resulting multimodal dataset was publicly released.

Conclusion: Separating cybersickness into nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation enables more informative continuous assessment during VR exposure than a single overall sickness measure. The approach appears feasible because it preserves usability and does not disrupt attention. The publicly available multimodal dataset may facilitate development and validation of future cybersickness models, although the findings are based on a VR-rollercoaster context and should be tested across broader VR experiences and populations.

Abstract: Cybersickness is often assessed during VR exposure through discrete overall sickness, which cannot capture specific symptoms or their changes over time. Post-exposure questionnaires such as the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire provide symptom-specific measures but cannot be collected during immersion. We investigate whether separating cybersickness into three symptom-specific dimensions -- nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation -- during immersion provides more informative continuous measurements. We conducted a between-subjects study with 52 participants in a VR-rollercoaster, comparing a three-slider interface with a single-slider interface. Concurrently, participants also completed an auditory Simon task to measure attention. We additionally collected multimodal set of physiological signals, including GSR, ECG, PPG, eye and head tracking. Results show that each slider correlated most strongly with its corresponding SSQ subscale, whereas the single slider correlated with all SSQ subscales. Using three sliders did not significantly affect usability, workload, or attention. We publicly release the multimodal dataset to support future cybersickness research.

### [2] [Breaking the Modality Barrier: Multimodal Fusion With Missing Modalities for Cybersickness Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07386)
*Purnata Saha,Rifatul Islam,John Quarles*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper presents a transformer-based cybersickness prediction framework that remains effective when arbitrary physiological or behavioral sensors are missing. It aligns heterogeneous sensor representations through eye-tracking-anchored contrastive learning and uses masked self-attention to fuse only available modalities. Across VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster, the model is highly robust to single-sensor dropout and generalizes well when trained jointly across datasets, achieving 0.51 MAE and 0.63 Pearson correlation.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Cybersickness substantially limits comfortable virtual-reality use, but accurate prediction commonly depends on a fixed collection of sensors. Existing multimodal models are therefore fragile under sensor failure or missing modalities and often do not transfer well across datasets collected with different hardware and sensor configurations. The study aims to develop a prediction method that can operate with arbitrary sensor subsets without imputing unavailable data and that generalizes across VR environments.

Method: The authors adapt a transformer architecture for variable-modality input. Each sensor modality is embedded separately, and anchor-based contrastive learning aligns heterogeneous embeddings in a shared latent space, using eye tracking as the anchor modality. During multimodal fusion, masked self-attention prevents unavailable sensors from contributing. The method is evaluated on three public datasets—VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster—with different VR systems and sensor configurations. Robustness is assessed under single-sensor dropout, and cross-dataset generalization is tested through joint training.

Result: Under single-sensor dropout, the prediction error changes by less than 0.09 MAE, indicating strong resilience to missing inputs. Joint training on all three datasets achieves 0.51 MAE and a Pearson correlation of 0.63, substantially outperforming models trained separately on individual datasets. The results support improved robustness and cross-dataset generalization.

Conclusion: The study demonstrates that arbitrary-modality transformer fusion, combined with anchor-based contrastive alignment and masked attention, can provide reliable cybersickness prediction despite sensor loss and dataset heterogeneity. Joint multi-dataset training further improves generalization across VR systems. The approach is promising for practical VR monitoring, although the abstract does not report details such as per-dataset results, statistical significance, computational cost, or performance under multiple simultaneous sensor failures.

Abstract: Cybersickness is a major barrier to comfortable VR use, and reliable prediction often requires multiple physiological and behavioral sensors. However, existing models typically assume a fixed set of modalities, making them vulnerable when sensors are unavailable and difficult to generalize across datasets with different sensor configurations. We adapt a transformer-based model that handles arbitrary sensor combinations without imputing missing data. Anchor-based contrastive learning maps heterogeneous sensor embeddings into a shared latent space using eye tracking as the anchor modality, while masked self-attention excludes unavailable sensors during fusion. We evaluate our approach on three publicly available cybersickness datasets: VR.net, SIM21, and Blur-Rollercoaster, collected using different VR systems and sensor configurations. Across single-sensor dropout conditions, prediction error changes by less than 0.09 MAE. Joint training across all datasets achieves 0.51 MAE and 0.63 Pearson correlation, substantially outperforming single-dataset models. These results demonstrate robust and generalizable cybersickness prediction across diverse VR systems.

### [3] [Thinking Is Not Writing: Designing AI Chatbots to Connect Reasoning and Revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07792)
*Lucile Favero,Tanja Käser,Juan Antonio Pérez-Ortiz,Nuria Oliver*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper presents Maike, a Socratic AI chatbot that helps secondary-school students improve argumentative writing by connecting idea-level reflection with revision. In a classroom study of 65 students, about 40% improved their essay scores, and Maike users showed more idea-focused dialogue and stronger discussion-to-revision links. <details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Many AI writing tools focus on generating or correcting text, but effective argumentative writing also requires students to reflect on the quality of their ideas and arguments and then apply that reflection during revision. The paper addresses the gap between conversational support and actual writing-process changes, as well as the need to evaluate educational AI through interaction patterns and learning outcomes rather than interface use alone.

Method: The authors developed Maike, a Socratic chatbot with argument diagnosis, targeted questions, and a student model. It supports two linked modes: Think, for discussing and reflecting on arguments, and Write, for revising the essay. A classroom study with 65 secondary-school students compared Maike with a control chatbot that used the same interface, language model, and Think/Write modes but lacked Maike’s distinctive support. The analysis examined essay-score changes, dialogue content, movement between discussion and editing, and links between discussed ideas and subsequent revisions.

Result: Approximately 40% of students improved their essay scores. Maike users devoted a larger share of their dialogue to ideas and arguments than control users (56% versus 44%) and moved iteratively between discussion and editing. Transitions in which ideas discussed with the chatbot were subsequently incorporated into revisions were positively associated with score gains. The findings indicate that interaction quality and integration with the writing process matter more than chatbot use alone.

Conclusion: AI writing support can be educationally valuable when it promotes reflection that directly informs revision, rather than generating essay text for students. Maike contributes an architecture for supporting argumentative reasoning and an analytical approach for relating chatbot interactions to writing processes and outcomes. The study’s main design implication is to deliberately coordinate idea-level dialogue with concrete editing activity.

Abstract: Supporting students' argumentative writing with AI requires connecting reflection to their revision processes. We present Maike, a Socratic chatbot supporting writing through argument diagnosis, targeted questioning, and a student model across Think and Write modes. In a classroom study, 65 secondary-school students used Maike or a control chatbot sharing the interface, language model, and Think/Write modes. Approximately 40% of students improved their essay scores. However, Maike users engaged more in idea-level dialogue (56% vs. 44%) and moved iteratively between discussion and editing. Transitions from ideas discussed in dialogue to revisions were positively associated with score gains. These findings suggest a design target for AI writing support: coordinating reflection with revision. We contribute an architecture for supporting argumentative reasoning without generating essay text and an approach linking interaction patterns to writing processes and outcomes. As AI becomes embedded in classrooms, understanding these connections is essential to assessing (and designing for) its educational value.

### [5] [Tasteply: Externalizing Stable and Situational Preference as Manipulable Spatial Objects](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07851)
*DaEun Choi,Daniel Lee*

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: Tasteply is an interactive recommender-system interface that makes latent preference structure visible and manipulable by representing stable preferences as a global field and situational preferences as spatial attractors. <details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Conventional recommender systems typically model user preference as a single, relatively undifferentiated signal. This overlooks the distinction between stable, enduring interests and situational preferences that emerge only under particular contexts. The paper is motivated by the need to externalize this hidden preference structure so users can understand and directly control how recommendations are generated.

Method: The authors introduce Tasteply, an interactive spatial representation of preference. Situational preferences are modeled as attractors in a manipulable space, while stable preference is represented as an underlying global field. Users steer recommendations by moving through the spatial environment and adjusting the strength of the global field. The concept was evaluated through an exploratory user study with five participants (N=5).

Result: The study found that the spatial control mechanism was effective for manipulating preference-based recommendations. It also identified limitations of the approach and opportunities for improvement. Because the evaluation involved only five participants and was exploratory, the findings provide initial evidence rather than broad generalizable validation.

Conclusion: Tasteply demonstrates a promising interaction paradigm for making latent recommender-system preferences observable and actionable. Separating stable and situational preferences may give users more nuanced control over recommendation outcomes, although further research with larger and more rigorous evaluations is needed to assess usability, effectiveness, and generalizability.

Abstract: Most recommender systems treat user preferences as a single signal to infer and optimize, yet human preferences are a mix of a stable preference and situational preferences that turn on and off depending on context. Through Tasteply, we externalize this preference structure as manipulable spatial objects. Tasteply represents each situational preference as an attractor and the stable preference as an underlying global field. Users can adjust their feed by moving around the space and adjusting the strength of the global field, turning the preference structure that remains latent within the model into something they can see and act on directly to steer the recommendation algorithm. Through an exploratory study (N=5), we found that this spatial control was effective, along with its limits and future direction as a means of manipulating preference-based recommendations.

### [7] [IntentWeaver: Sustaining Coherence across Evolving Intents in Human-AI Co-Writing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08214)

Main category: cs.HC

TL;DR: The paper identifies intent incoherence in human–AI co-writing and introduces IntentWeaver, a system that maintains alignment between high-level writing goals and concrete text changes. In a within-subjects study with 24 participants, IntentWeaver improved higher-level intent revision, reduced unnoticed deviations, and produced text that better fulfilled writers’ goals.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Writers’ intentions are hierarchical: broad purposes guide increasingly concrete choices in wording and structure. Generative AI produces dense drafts that can capture writers’ attention and encourage local edits, making them less likely to revisit higher-level goals. As a result, lower-level text changes may gradually diverge from broader intentions without being noticed or reconciled. The paper addresses this breakdown, termed intent incoherence, to support more reflective and coherent human–AI writing.

Method: The authors first conducted a formative study tracing how intent incoherence develops, from attention capture by AI-generated drafts to unnoticed intent drift and failed reconciliation. They then developed IntentWeaver, which infers an intent hierarchy from the draft and writer interactions, assesses each intent’s fulfillment and coherence with intents at other levels whenever the text changes, and propagates the effects of changes across the hierarchy. The system was evaluated in a within-subjects study with 24 participants against a baseline condition.

Result: Participants using IntentWeaver revisited and refined higher-level intents more frequently, experienced fewer unnoticed deviations between their goals and the evolving text, and produced writing that more effectively realized those intents than with the baseline.

Conclusion: IntentWeaver demonstrates that explicitly modeling and monitoring hierarchical intents can help sustain coherence in human–AI co-writing. The findings suggest that co-writing tools should support ongoing intent evolution—not merely preserve initially stated goals—and should account for emergent intents that arise during writing.

Abstract: Writers hold intents at many levels, from overarching goals to concrete wording, and evolve them by periodically popping back up from the text to higher-level intents. Our formative study traced this breakdown from attention capture through silent drift to failed reconciliation. We present IntentWeaver, which supports intent evolution while sustaining coherence in human-AI co-writing. It derives an intent hierarchy from the draft and writer interactions, evaluates each intent's fulfillment and cross-level coherence at every change, and propagates consequences across intent levels. In a within-subjects study (N=24) against a baseline, participants using IntentWeaver revisited and evolved higher-level intents more often, with fewer unnoticed deviations, and produced texts better realizing them. We further discuss design implications for emergent intents.
