Motivation: Existing AI-use labels disclose that AI was involved but do not adequately communicate how creators and AI contributed to the final image. Editing logs contain richer evidence—requests, suggestions, and revisions—but it is unclear how creators interpret these records when assigning contribution. The study therefore investigates how spatially linked provenance and interaction summaries can support contribution judgment and explanation.

Method: The authors developed Roop, a mixed-initiative design probe that links edited image regions to their corresponding editing-history events (spatial provenance) and summarizes interaction activity with a heat map. Twenty participants edited images, annotated the contributions of humans and AI, and then reviewed the provenance visualization. Their attribution judgments and reflections on the editing process were analyzed.

Result: Participants generally credited themselves with directing or intending changes, while crediting the AI with selecting or determining visual details. Contribution judgments were influenced by whether participants felt their interactions successfully achieved their intentions. Reviewing the provenance heat map helped participants recall prior interactions, but it did not consistently alter their attribution judgments.

Conclusion: AI-editing provenance systems should preserve not only a record of actions, but also sufficient contextual information to explain how human–AI interactions shaped the result. Spatial provenance and interaction summaries can support reflection and communication, although provenance alone may not substantially change perceptions of contribution.

Abstract: Images co-edited with AI are often labeled to disclose AI use. However, creators feel these labels do not sufficiently describe their contributions to the editing process. Editing histories preserve requests, suggestions, and revisions, but how do creators use these logs to judge contribution? We investigated this with Roop, a mixed-initiative design probe that links image regions to editing histories (spatial provenance) and summarizes interactions in a heat map. Twenty participants edited images, annotated human and AI contributions, then reviewed the spatial provenance heat map. Participants credited themselves for directing changes while crediting AI for determining visual details. They judged contribution partly by whether their interactions achieved their intentions. Reviewing provenance helped participants remember interactions without always changing whom they credited. These findings motivate provenance systems that preserve enough context for creators to explain how interactions shaped their work, alongside the record of what happened.
